There is a window in the day, around half past four, when the light over the estate turns the colour of the butter we churn. It is the hour the herd comes in.

The path to the dairy shed runs past the lily ponds. By late afternoon the pads have opened fully, and the walk — about eight minutes from the rooms — is an unhurried one, the heat already loosening its grip. Somewhere along the way the air changes: warmer straw, the low sound of the herd, the unmistakable smell of a working farm at the end of its day.

## What you actually do there

Not much. You stand. The dairy hand greets you, hands you a small steel measure, and you watch — or, if you've asked, you try.

The cows are large and entirely unbothered. They look at you the way someone you've just sat next to on a train looks at you — polite, curious, mildly amused. You pull. The milk arrives. It is warm, and it carries a small foam on top.

> You haven't tasted milk like this before. There's no comparison to make.

## Where the milk goes

What is drawn at the afternoon milking is bottled fresh the same evening and chilled overnight. By the time the coffee is poured at breakfast the next morning, it is the milk from yesterday's golden hour — never more than a few hours old, never from anywhere but the field you can see from your room.

It is a small thing, this walk. Optional, never scheduled too tightly, easy to miss. But the guests who make it back tend to talk about it more than the safari.
