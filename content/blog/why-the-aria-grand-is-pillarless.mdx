There is a particular pause that happens when a wedding planner walks into the Aria Grand for the first time. They look up — at a 25-foot ceiling — and then they walk a slow, deliberate circle of the floor. Three-quarters of the way around, they stop. And they nod.

What they're looking for is the columns. There aren't any.

## The constraint a column creates

A column inside a banquet hall is not just an architectural fact. It is a planning problem.

It dictates where you can put the mandap. It blocks sightlines from the back of the seated area. It interrupts the photographer's wide. It makes table layouts a compromise between "where would we put it ideally" and "where can we put it given the structure." In rooms with multiple columns, planners spend the first hour of a site visit just walking the floor with masking tape, marking what's actually usable.

In a 9,500 square foot space, even one column in the wrong spot can lose you fifteen percent of practical capacity.

## What pillarless costs

A lot, structurally. The roof spans the full width of the hall in a single, engineered truss — the kind of beam-and-cable system you usually see in airport terminals or amphitheatres. The slab depth on a pillarless hall is significantly thicker than a column-supported one. The materials cost more. The build takes longer.

The cost is borne once, by the resort. The benefit is borne every weekend, by every family who hosts a wedding here.

> A pillarless hall does not look more special. It works more special. The difference shows up in the photographs.

## What it lets us do

A few specific things, in order of how often planners ask about them:

- **Mandap placement.** You can position the mandap anywhere — centred, off-axis, against the long wall — without an engineering compromise. We have hosted weddings with the mandap directly under the central spotlight, fifty feet from the nearest support.
- **Capacity that matches the floor plan.** With its vast attached lawn, the Aria Grand scales to gatherings of up to 2,000 guests. That number is not theoretical. It is what the layout actually permits.
- **Drapery and lighting.** A pillarless hall lets a lighting designer rig from the ceiling without compromises. Sweeping fabric, suspended floral, projection mapping — all of these work cleanly when there isn't a column in the way of the rigging point you wanted.
- **Photography.** The wide shots become genuinely wide. Drone footage from the lawn into the hall doesn't catch a pillar mid-frame. It is the kind of detail you don't notice until you've seen the photographs of a wedding that didn't have it.

## When pillarless doesn't matter

To be fair: for a 200-person sangeet on a soft lawn evening, none of this matters. You'd be at Cocktail Lawn or Sudesh II, and a column would never have been in your way.

Pillarless matters when scale meets intent. A 1,500-guest reception with a mandap, a stage, multiple food counters, a working bar, photographers and videographers all needing space to move — that is when the absence of one inconvenient column becomes the reason your weekend went smoothly.

## And the smaller siblings

Across from the Aria Grand sit the Aria sub-spaces — Aria I + II + Deck (3,500 sq ft) and Aria III (6,000 sq ft) — also pillarless, designed for mid-sized functions where you want the same column-free freedom at a smaller footprint. A board meeting can comfortably take Aria III; a 400-guest reception can take Aria I + II + Deck; the headline ceremony fills the full Grand. All three connect, so the same wedding can use all three across a weekend.

It is not a small architectural choice. It is the difference between a hall that can be decorated and a hall that gets out of the way of what you want to celebrate.
