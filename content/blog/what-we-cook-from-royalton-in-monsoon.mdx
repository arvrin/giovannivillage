The kitchen changes its menu twice a year. Once in October, when the long mango and citrus arc gives way to the winter greens. And once in late June, when the first proper rain breaks and the farm changes inside a fortnight.

Most of what we cook comes from Royalton Farms, the working organic farm inside the estate. That means the menu doesn't get to be the same year-round. We don't pretend.

## What disappears

The summer mangoes — Alphonso, Dasheri, Langra — finish by mid-June. The kitchen gets a last small batch from Royalton's older trees, and then the slow Malwa season arrives. Tomatoes, our own, stop being as sweet — they become sharper, more useful for a chutney than a salad. The summer cucumbers fade. The hibiscus blooms taper.

For about a week in early July, the menu reads thin if you've been here often. That is by design. We could buy our way through it. We don't.

## What only shows up now

This is the part regulars wait for.

- **Bhutta.** Sweetcorn arrives green and dense. The kitchen does a slow charred-corn salad with chaat masala and house yoghurt, and a corn-and-leek soup in the evenings that has accidentally become a small cult dish.
- **Wild mushrooms.** The forest edges around Ratapani produce a small annual flush after the second proper rain. We forage carefully and conservatively, with the naturalist. They go into a single butter-and-garlic preparation at Gourmet By The Woods that's the dish to ask for that week.
- **Karela ka achaar.** Bitter gourd from the farm, sundried in the only fortnight you can do it, pickled into the sharp, slow chutney that comes out at breakfast.
- **Singhada.** Water chestnut from the lake side. Roasted and lightly salted, served at the bar with sundowners.
- **Hara channa.** Green chickpeas in the pod, served at table for guests to shell and snack on while the rest of the meal arrives.

> The best meal of the monsoon is the one that wasn't planned in February. It is the one the rain itself put on the plate.

## How it changes the breakfast

Royalton's eggs taste different in monsoon. The hens eat differently when the grass is wet. The yolks deepen. The whites set firmer in the pan.

We start cracking them an hour later than in summer, because the kitchen team and the dairy hand stand together at five-thirty waiting for the rain to ease enough to fetch the morning milk. The coffee is the same, the bread is the same, but a soft monsoon scramble with the day's eggs and a corner of sundried tomato is the kind of small thing you'd remember for a year.

## What this all means for a stay

We don't promise consistency the way a city hotel does. We promise honesty. The menu is the truth of what the farm just gave us. If you visit in three different seasons, you eat three different meals.

That is not a marketing line. It is just how a working organic farm shapes a kitchen, when you let it.

If you are planning a monsoon stay, ask the chef in advance about the foraged mushroom dish. It is offered for about ten days a year, and we do not advertise the date.
